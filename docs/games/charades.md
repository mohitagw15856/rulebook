## How many players changes what

| Players | Setup | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **4-6** | Two teams of two or three. Everybody writes two or three slips. | Works, but each person performs constantly, which is exhausting for the shy and wonderful for the rest. |
| **7-12** | Two teams. Aim for around three slips per person in the bowl. | The best range. Long enough between turns to recover, short enough that nobody is bored. |
| **13-20** | Three or four teams rotating, otherwise the wait becomes unbearable. | Above about fourteen, consider splitting into two entirely separate games in different rooms. |

## Rules

One person acts. Nobody speaks. Everybody shouts. It has survived for two
centuries because it needs nothing but people and paper.

## Preparing the bowl

Everyone writes two or three titles or phrases on separate slips — films,
books, songs, television programmes, well-known sayings. Fold them and put them
all in one container.

Agree what is fair before anybody writes. A group that has never established
this will produce at least one clue nobody present has heard of, and the
resulting argument is more predictable than the game.

## Performing

Draw a slip and read it silently. Set the timer, usually to a minute or two.

Before acting, it is conventional to indicate the category and the structure:
how many words, which word you are tackling, and how many syllables it has.
There is a traditional vocabulary of gestures for this, and every group's
version differs slightly.

While acting you may not speak, mouth words, make any sound, or point at an
object in the room to indicate the word. Miming the *idea* of an object is the
game; pointing at the actual thing is not.

## Guessing

Your team calls out guesses freely, as many as they like. There is no penalty
for a wrong guess and no turn order — it is meant to be a scramble.

If they get it before the timer, the team scores. If not, most groups reveal the
answer, which is usually the funniest moment of the round.

## Scoring

One point per successful guess, and play alternates between teams. Keep score if
you want to. Most groups start doing so and quietly stop, which is fine — the
game does not depend on it.

## Settle the argument

### Can you point at something in the room to indicate a word?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

No. Pointing at a real object to convey the word for it bypasses the whole activity. You may mime handling an imaginary object freely — the distinction is between representing a thing and simply indicating one.

*What it changes:* Groups that allow it find every clue containing a common noun becomes trivial, which flattens the game quickly.

```console
$ rulebook ruling charades "can I point at things"
```

### Is "sounds like" allowed?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

Yes, and the traditional signal is tugging your earlobe. You then act out a word that rhymes with the one you actually need. It is standard in nearly every tradition, though a few groups ban it for being too powerful.

```console
$ rulebook ruling charades "sounds like gesture"
```

### Can you mouth the word silently?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

No. Forming the word with your lips is treated exactly as speaking it, even with no sound. This is the most frequently broken rule in the game, usually accidentally and in the last ten seconds.

```console
$ rulebook ruling charades "can I mouth it"
```

### Does a guess count if they say the right words in the wrong order?

**Not an official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

There is no governing body, so no answer is official. The common convention is that the performer decides, and their decision stands.

*The house version:* Stricter groups require the exact title. Looser groups accept anything that demonstrates the guesser clearly knows the answer.

*What it changes:* Strictness matters more than which rule you pick — a table that switches standards halfway through will argue about it for the rest of the evening.

```console
$ rulebook ruling charades "close enough guess"
```

### Do you have to signal the number of words before acting?

**Official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

It is conventional rather than compulsory, and skipping it makes the round substantially harder. Most groups treat the word count and category as a courtesy the performer owes their own team.

```console
$ rulebook ruling charades "do I show how many words"
```

## Teaching it

Two minutes, and almost everyone claims to already know it. Teach it anyway,
because the disagreements are always about the same three things.

**The core is one sentence:** "You act it out, you can't make a sound, your team
guesses."

**Then immediately settle the three arguments before they happen.** These are
the only rules disputes charades ever produces, and naming them takes twenty
seconds:

1. "No pointing at things in the room." Someone will point at a lamp for the
   word *light*.
2. "Sounds-like is allowed." Or is not — decide now, out loud.
3. "If nobody knows the film, that's the writer's fault, not the actor's."

**Demonstrate the opening gestures rather than listing them.** Hold up three
fingers for three words, then one finger for the first word, then two fingers on
your forearm for two syllables. Ten seconds of showing replaces a page of
description, and every group's gestures differ slightly anyway.

**Say the thing that makes shy people comfortable:** "You can pass and put it
back. Nobody minds." One sentence, and it changes who is willing to play.

**Set the timer generously the first round.** Two minutes rather than one.
Nothing kills the mood faster than the first three performers all failing.

**Tell people what to write when they fill the bowl.** "Something you'd expect
most people here to have heard of." Without that instruction you will get an
obscure Norwegian film and a forty-second silence, and the round after it will
be quieter than the one before.

## Variants worth knowing

**Whole-room guessing** — Drop the teams and let anyone guess. Chaotic, much faster, and better for groups where people are still learning each other's names.

**Written by the other team** — Each team writes the slips their opponents will perform. Produces far more obscure and far funnier clues, and requires trust.

## When it is fair to stop

It ends when the room's energy drops, not at a score. Somebody should say "last round each" out loud rather than letting it fizzle, because charades that stops cleanly is remembered far better than charades that trails off.

## When a piece goes missing

There is nothing to substitute. Paper, a bowl and a phone timer are the whole requirement, and any of the three can be improvised.

## Accessibility

It is a physical game and excludes anyone who cannot perform standing movement, though seated charades works perfectly well if the group agrees to it. It is also loud and fast, which can be genuinely difficult for players who need time to process speech. Allowing a player to pass without penalty removes most of the social pressure.

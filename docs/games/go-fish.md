## Rules

A children's classic that is quietly a game of deduction: every question you ask
tells everyone else what you are holding.

## Setup

Deal five cards to each player, or seven when only two or three are playing.
Spread the rest face down in the middle — this is the pond.

## Playing a turn

Ask **one specific player** for **one specific rank**: "Priya, do you have any
sevens?"

You must already hold at least one card of that rank. This is the rule that
makes the game work, and the one most often dropped.

- If they hold any, they must hand over **all** of them, and you take another
  turn.
- If they hold none, they say "go fish" and you draw one card from the pond.
  Your turn ends.

## Completing sets

The moment you hold all four cards of a rank, place them face up in front of
you. That set is scored and out of play.

## Ending the game

The game ends when all thirteen sets have been laid down. Whoever has the most
sets wins; equal sets is a shared win.

If your hand empties while cards remain in the pond, draw one and continue. You
are only out when both your hand and the pond are empty.

## Settle the argument

### Can you ask for a card you do not already have?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

No. You must already hold at least one card of the rank you ask for. This is what makes the game a deduction puzzle rather than a guessing game — every question you ask tells the table something about your hand.

```console
$ rulebook ruling go-fish "can you ask for any card go fish"
```

### Do you get another turn if the other player hands cards over?

**Official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

Yes. A successful ask earns you another turn, and you keep going until somebody tells you to go fish. Groups that skip this rule make the game considerably slower and flatter.

```console
$ rulebook ruling go-fish "do you go again in go fish"
```

### What if you draw the exact card you asked for?

**Not an official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

The traditional game simply ends your turn.

*The house version:* Many groups let you show the card and take another turn as a reward for the lucky draw.

*What it changes:* Small, cheerful, and popular with children. It lengthens turns slightly.

```console
$ rulebook ruling go-fish "draw the card you asked for"
```

### What happens when you run out of cards but the pond is not empty?

**Official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

Draw a new card from the pond and carry on. You are only out of the game when both your hand and the pond are empty.

```console
$ rulebook ruling go-fish "out of cards go fish"
```

## Teaching it

## Say this

"Ask someone for a card you already have. If they have it, they give you all of
them and you go again. If not, they say go fish and you take one from the
middle. Four of a kind goes down in front of you."

Deal and play. There is nothing else.

## Emphasise exactly one rule

**You have to already hold the rank you ask for.** Children skip this
constantly, and it is the rule that turns the game from guessing into thinking.

## For older players

Say once, quietly, near the start: "Listen to what people ask for." That single
nudge converts the game from luck to memory, and is the difference between a
game a nine-year-old tolerates and one they want to play again.

## When a piece goes missing

Works with any deck. With a partial deck, remove whole ranks rather than individual cards so that every set is still completable.

## Accessibility

Rank-based rather than colour-based, so colour vision is not needed. Holding a large fan of cards is the main dexterity barrier — a card holder or a stand solves it for small hands and for anyone with limited grip.

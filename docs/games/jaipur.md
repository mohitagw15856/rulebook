## Rules

Two traders, one market of five cards, and a shrinking pile of tokens. The whole
game is knowing when to sell.

## Setup

Shuffle the deck. Place three camels face up in the market plus two more cards,
making a market of five. Deal five cards to each player; any camels dealt to you
go face up in front of you immediately, into what is called your herd.

Lay out the goods tokens in stacks, highest value on top. Diamonds, gold and
silver are the expensive commodities; cloth, spice and leather are the cheap
ones.

## Your turn — take or sell

**Taking** comes in three forms:

- Take one single goods card from the market into your hand.
- Take all the camels from the market into your herd, however many there are.
- Swap two or more cards, exchanging cards from your hand and herd for the same
  number from the market.

**Selling** means discarding any number of cards of one commodity and taking
that many tokens from the top of that commodity's stack.

Because tokens descend in value, selling early gets you the good ones. Selling
late gets you whatever is left.

## The rules that shape everything

You may never hold more than seven cards in hand. Camels do not count towards
this, because they live in your herd rather than your hand.

Diamonds, gold and silver may only be sold two or more at a time. You cannot
sell a single one.

Selling three, four or five cards at once earns a bonus token drawn blind from
the matching stack, and those bonuses are large enough to decide rounds.

## Ending a round

The round ends immediately when three token stacks are empty, or when the deck
cannot refill the market.

Total your tokens. Whoever has the larger herd of camels takes the five-point
camel bonus. Higher total wins the round.

## The match

First to two rounds wins.

## The tension

Taking camels is powerful because it lets you swap greedily later, but every
camel you take is one you are not selling. And every turn you spend building a
big set is a turn your opponent might spend taking the top diamond token.

## Settle the argument

### Do camels count towards your hand limit?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

No. Camels sit face up in your herd, separate from your hand, and are exempt from the seven-card limit entirely. Taking every camel in the market never costs you hand space.

```console
$ rulebook ruling jaipur "camel hand limit"
```

### Can you take just some of the camels?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

No. Taking camels means taking every camel currently in the market, and the market is then refilled from the deck. Leaving one behind is not an option.

*What it changes:* This makes camels a timing decision — taking them refills the market for your opponent, which is sometimes exactly what you do not want.

```console
$ rulebook ruling jaipur "taking camels"
```

### Can you sell a single diamond?

**Official rule.** Played by almost everyone, almost everywhere.

No. Diamonds, gold and silver must be sold two or more at a time. Cheap goods — cloth, spice and leather — may be sold one at a time. A player holding a single diamond and nothing else is genuinely stuck with it.

```console
$ rulebook ruling jaipur "selling one diamond"
```

### Can you use camels in a swap?

**Official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

Yes, and it is the main reason to collect them. A swap exchanges cards from your hand and herd combined for the same number from the market, so a large herd lets you take several valuable goods in one action.

```console
$ rulebook ruling jaipur "swapping with camels"
```

### What ends a round?

**Official rule.** Widespread but far from universal.

Three goods token stacks running empty, or the deck being unable to refill the market. It ends immediately at that point, so deliberately emptying a third stack while ahead is a legitimate way to close out a round.

```console
$ rulebook ruling jaipur "when does a jaipur round end"
```

### Who gets the camel bonus if both players have the same number?

**Official rule.** Occasional, or specific to one group.

Nobody. The five-point camel token is awarded only to the player with strictly more camels, and a tie means it goes unclaimed for that round.

```console
$ rulebook ruling jaipur "camel bonus tie"
```

## Teaching it

Five minutes, and it teaches better through one demonstration round than through
explanation.

**Frame the whole game in one sentence:** "You're collecting goods and selling
them, and the earlier you sell something the more it's worth."

**Show the token stacks and point at the descending numbers.** "See how these go
seven, seven, five, five, five? Whoever sells diamonds first gets the sevens."
That single observation is the game's economy and it lands instantly when they
can see the numbers.

**Then the turn, as a fork:** "Every turn you either take cards or sell cards.
Never both."

**Explain the three ways to take, but demonstrate the swap.** Taking one card and
taking camels are obvious. Swapping is the one people forget exists, and it is
the most powerful action in the game. Do one in front of them.

**Explain camels as a resource, not a commodity:** "You never sell camels. They
sit in front of you and they're what lets you do big swaps. Also, whoever has
more at the end gets five points."

**Say the two restrictions plainly,** because both will otherwise be discovered
mid-turn: "Seven cards in hand maximum — camels don't count. And you can't sell
a single diamond, gold or silver; you need at least two."

**Point out the bonus tokens and let the implication land:** "Sell three at once
and you get an extra token. Sell five and you get a much bigger one." Then say
the strategic tension out loud: "So do you sell two diamonds now for the sevens,
or wait for a third and hope they're still there?" That question is Jaipur.

**Mention it is best of three** so nobody treats the first round as the whole
game and plays it recklessly.

## When it is fair to stop

Each round takes about ten minutes and the match is best of three, so there is no reason to abandon one. A player who has lost the first two rounds has lost the match already and the third need not be played.

## When a piece goes missing

The tokens are the scoring system and their descending values matter, so they cannot be replaced casually — though a written list of the values would do at a pinch. The cards are the game.

## Accessibility

Commodities are distinguished by both colour and illustration, which works well. The tokens are small and stack into piles that need reading from the top down, which is the main visual demand. Turns are short and the whole thing sits in a small space, making it excellent for cramped tables.
